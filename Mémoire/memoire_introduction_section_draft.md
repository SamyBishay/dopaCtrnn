# Memoire Introduction Section

## 1.1 Goal-directed and habitual control of behaviour

Adaptive behaviour is widely held to arise from at least two dissociable systems for instrumental control. A goal-directed system selects actions by representing their consequences and the current value of those consequences; it is flexible and sensitive to changes in the action–outcome contingency and to outcome devaluation. A habitual system, by contrast, selects actions through learned stimulus–response associations; it is fast and automatic but inflexible and, by its operational definition, insensitive to outcome devaluation. The same division is captured computationally by the distinction between model-based control, which plans over an internal model of the environment, and model-free control, which caches action values from experience (Gläscher et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2014). The two systems are at least partly dissociable in the brain: prefrontal and dorsomedial circuits support flexible, goal-directed control, dorsolateral striatal circuits support habitual control, and individual differences in prefrontal versus striatal dopamine track reliance on the two strategies (Doll et al., 2016).

A central, well-replicated observation is that control shifts from the goal-directed to the habitual system as a behaviour is repeated: with overtraining, an action that was initially sensitive to devaluation becomes automatic and devaluation-insensitive. Recording across cortex and striatum during this transition, Smith and Graybiel (2013) found that habit formation is accompanied by the emergence of task-bracketing activity in both the infralimbic cortex and the dorsolateral striatum, the two regions operating as coordinated "dual operators" rather than the striatum acting alone. How control is allocated between the systems has in turn motivated arbitration accounts, in which control is assigned to whichever system is currently the more reliable (Lee et al., 2014). The transition is, more broadly, one instance of a general principle that neural populations reorganise their representations as a task is learned (Echeveste et al., 2020; Yaghoubi et al., 2026).

## 1.2 From migration to reversible handoff: the puzzle posed by Villet et al. (2025)

The classical interpretation of this shift is one of *information migration*, or consolidation: as a habit forms, the locus of control — and, on stronger readings, the underlying memory itself — moves from cortical to striatal circuits, and the cortical contribution is progressively supplanted. On this view, once a behaviour is habitual the cortical solution is no longer needed and, in the limit, no longer available.

Villet et al. (2025) provide a direct challenge to that prediction. Using a delayed non-match-to-place (DNMTP) T-maze in mice, with chemogenetic inhibition of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) across learning and maintenance, they characterised the transition through several informative manipulations. During early learning, behaviour was goal-directed and devaluation-sensitive and depended on the mPFC: silencing mPFC broke learning, whereas silencing DLS had no effect. After overtraining, the same behaviour was automated and devaluation-insensitive, and the dependencies reversed, with DLS rather than mPFC now mattering. The decisive result, however, was what silencing the DLS *did* during the maintenance phase. Rather than abolishing performance, it re-engaged the goal-directed strategy, devaluation-sensitivity included; and silencing mPFC and DLS together collapsed performance entirely. The cortical solution had therefore not been deleted or overwritten during habit formation. It had gone *dormant* and remained reactivable, and both systems remained functionally necessary even after the behaviour was automated.

This pattern is difficult to reconcile with a migration account. If the cortical solution were supplanted, silencing the striatum in an overtrained animal should not recover a devaluation-sensitive, goal-directed strategy on demand. The reversibility of the handoff implies instead that the cortical solution is retained intact throughout overtraining, and that something *gates which system is expressed* rather than transferring a solution from one region to another. The mechanistic puzzle is therefore the following: what allows a learned cortical controller to be switched off as a habit takes over, yet switched back on rapidly when the habitual controller is removed? A satisfying answer must preserve the cortical weights, suppress their expression during habitual control, and restore that expression quickly — and it must do so as a consequence of learning, since the animal is never told when to hand control over.

## 1.3 Dopamine as a mechanism for gating control

We argue that dopamine is well suited to this gating role, for three reasons that jointly motivate the model developed here.

First, dopamine supports two *independent* learning signals, only one of which carries reward. Beyond its classical role as a reward prediction error that drives value-based learning (Schultz, 2016), recent work shows that movement-related dopamine in the tail of the striatum encodes an *action* prediction error (APE) — a mismatch between the expected and the executed action — that reinforces stimulus–action associations without representing reward at all (Greenstreet et al., 2025). Reward and action prediction errors operate in parallel across different striatal territories, allowing value-based and value-free learning to proceed at the same time. A habitual controller trained by APE is, as a result, *structurally* devaluation-insensitive: never having represented reward, it cannot change its behaviour when reward is devalued. This furnishes a mechanistic, rather than merely descriptive, account of the defining property of habits.

Second, dopamine implements an adaptive, opponent control of which striatal pathway drives behaviour. In the OpAL\* framework (Jaskir & Frank, 2023), two opponent striatal actors — a D1/Go pathway that learns the benefits of actions and a D2/NoGo pathway that learns their costs — are combined in opposition, and the system optimises a *policy* rather than learning veridical reward values. Dynamic dopamine then amplifies the benefit pathway in rich environments and the cost pathway in lean ones, switching the system's effective strategy with no change in the learned weights. This single adaptive-dopamine move can be repurposed: if dopamine is held high while a goal-directed controller is needed and driven down once a habitual controller becomes competent, then an OpAL\*-style reduction in dopamine — run across an overtraining trajectory rather than across environments — can implement exactly the cortico-striatal transfer of control that Villet et al. observed. Opponent striatal dopamine of this kind is increasingly understood as a general substrate for cost–benefit control (Westbrook & Frank, 2025).

Third, dopamine acts on multiple timescales and can modulate the *expression* of learned circuitry independently of learning itself. Phasic and tonic dopamine engage D1- and D2-type receptors differentially according to their affinity (Dreyer et al., 2010; Grace, 1991), giving a single neuromodulator both a fast and a slow mode of action; in prefrontal cortex, D1 and D2 receptors exert opposing, state-dependent effects on the stability of working-memory representations (Kutter et al., 2026; Seamans & Yang, 2004). Most directly relevant, Hamid et al. (2021b) showed that dopamine plays a dual role of *building* latent behavioural attractors through plasticity and *revealing* them through neuromodulation of excitability, so that a learned structure can remain silent until dopamine's modulatory action expresses it. Dopamine release is itself spatially and temporally structured, with wave-like dynamics that route credit toward the striatal subregion responsible for an outcome (Hamid et al., 2021a) and region-specific signals that need not be uniform across the striatum (Engel et al., 2024). The general lesson is that dopamine can gate whether an intact, learned solution is currently expressed — precisely the operation a reversible handoff requires, and one consistent with views of accumbal dopamine as an instrument of cognitive control rather than a purely reflexive signal (Lloyd & Dayan, 2023).

Taken together, these properties suggest a single mechanism. If (i) dopamine sets a multiplicative gain on the *expression* of intact learned weights, (ii) the habitual system learns value-free by action prediction error, and (iii) dopamine is adaptively reduced as the habitual system becomes competent, then a network should reproduce both the goal-directed-to-habitual transition and its reversibility — the dormant cortical solution being one whose expression has been turned down, not erased, and which a restoration of dopaminergic drive can bring back.

## 1.4 The present study

We test this proposal in a minimal, biologically constrained model: a two-area recurrent network performing the same DNMTP T-maze, in which a goal-directed system (mPFC/DMS) and a habitual system (DLS) compete for control under dopaminergic gating. The model commits to three properties drawn from the argument above. (1) *Expression-level gain*: dopamine multiplicatively scales the output activation of the goal-directed system, r_out = gain(DA) · tanh(h), modulating how strongly intact learned activations are expressed rather than altering the weights themselves — the property that makes an instant fallback possible. (2) *A value-free habitual learner*: the habitual system is trained by a value-free behavioral signal (approximating an action prediction error in the spirit of Greenstreet et al., 2025) and never by reward, which makes its devaluation-insensitivity structural. (3) *An emergent handoff*: control transfers because the goal-directed system is under continual pressure to minimise its own dopamine demand and can do so safely only once the habitual system carries the policy, so that no rule schedules the transition. Two further asymmetries make the systems functionally distinct: the goal-directed system receives the full 6-dimensional observation including normalised spatial coordinates (affording location-based planning), whereas the habitual system receives only the 4-dimensional phase-signal observation with position withheld and therefore cannot plan the non-match rule (a deliberate modelling assumption); and the two systems are distinguished by phasic- versus tonic-dopamine sensitivity within the goal-directed network.

Our objectives are, accordingly, to determine whether such a network (a) learns the task; (b) shows an emergent, competence-locked transfer of control from the goal-directed to the habitual system; (c) exhibits Villet's devaluation dissociation and lesion-by-phase pattern; and (d) reproduces the reactivation result, the signature of a genuinely reversible handoff. We further ask whether the controlling system maintains a readable working-memory representation across the delay, as the task demands.

This yields three theoretical hypotheses, operationalised in the Method. First, the handoff is *emergent rather than scheduled*: control should transfer to the habitual system as, and only as, that system becomes competent, so that the timing of the transfer tracks each network's learning speed rather than a fixed point in training. Second, the two systems are *differentially sensitive to value*: devaluation should impair goal-directed control but leave habitual control intact, by construction of the value-free learning rule. Third, the handoff is *reversible*: silencing the habitual system after overtraining should preserve performance, raise the goal-directed system's dopamine demand, and restore devaluation-sensitivity, as the dormant cortical solution re-expresses. This third hypothesis is also the model's principal point of falsification: if the goal-directed system's dopamine demand fails to rise when the habitual system is removed, the apparent handoff would be a schedule in disguise, and we commit in advance to reporting such an outcome as a negative result.

The contribution of this work is to offer a mechanistic, reproducible account of a phenomenon that the dominant migration view does not naturally explain — the *reversibility* of cortico-striatal control transfer. By locating the transition in the dopaminergic expression of intact weights, and the habit's value-insensitivity in a value-free learning rule, the model treats the goal-directed-to-habitual shift and its instant reversal as two faces of a single dopamine mechanism. Should it succeed, it yields directly testable predictions: most pointedly, that the timing of the behavioural transition should track the competence of the habitual system, and that experimentally removing habitual control should reinstate the dormant goal-directed strategy together with its dopaminergic and value-sensitivity signatures.
