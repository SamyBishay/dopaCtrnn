# Memoire Introduction Section

## 1.1 Goal-directed and habitual control of behaviour

Adaptive behaviour is widely held to arise from at least two dissociable systems for instrumental control. A goal-directed system selects actions by representing their consequences and the current value of those consequences; it is flexible and sensitive to changes in the action–outcome contingency and to outcome devaluation. A habitual system, by contrast, selects actions through learned stimulus–response associations; it is fast and automatic but inflexible and, by its operational definition, insensitive to outcome devaluation. The same division is captured computationally by the distinction between model-based control, which plans over an internal model of the environment, and model-free control, which caches action values from experience (Gläscher et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2014). The two systems are at least partly dissociable in the brain: prefrontal and dorsomedial circuits support flexible, goal-directed control, dorsolateral striatal circuits support habitual control, and individual differences in prefrontal versus striatal dopamine track reliance on the two strategies (Doll et al., 2016).

A central, well-replicated observation is that control shifts from the goal-directed to the habitual system as a behaviour is repeated: with overtraining, an action that was initially sensitive to devaluation becomes automatic and devaluation-insensitive. Recording across cortex and striatum during this transition, Smith and Graybiel (2013) found that habit formation is accompanied by the emergence of task-bracketing activity in both the infralimbic cortex and the dorsolateral striatum, the two regions operating as coordinated "dual operators" rather than the striatum acting alone. How control is allocated between the systems has in turn motivated arbitration accounts, in which control is assigned to whichever system is currently the more reliable (Lee et al., 2014). One account of why habitual control is fast and automatic proposes that it rests on low-rank, stereotyped representations in recurrent circuits — a structural property that makes habitual responses both efficient and resistant to flexible updating (Echeveste et al., 2020). The transition is, more broadly, one instance of a general principle that neural populations reorganise their representations as a task is learned (Yaghoubi et al., 2026).

## 1.2 From migration to reversible handoff: the puzzle posed by Villet et al. (2025)

The classical interpretation of this shift is one of *information migration*, or consolidation: as a habit forms, the locus of control — and, on stronger readings, the underlying memory itself — moves from cortical to striatal circuits, and the cortical contribution is progressively supplanted. On this view, once a behaviour is habitual the cortical solution is no longer needed and, in the limit, no longer available.

Villet et al. (2025) provide a direct challenge to that prediction. Mice learned a delayed non-match-to-place (DNMTP) task in a T-maze: on each trial, one arm was designated the sample arm; after a 90-second delay during which the animal was held in a start box, it was returned to the junction and had to choose the *opposite* arm (the non-match rule) to receive a sucrose reward. Over days of training, mice transitioned from a learning phase (criterion: ≥70% correct over two non-consecutive days) to a maintenance phase (criterion: ≥80% correct over three consecutive days), during which the same behaviour became automatic and devaluation-insensitive (unreduced by sucrose pre-feeding). To dissect the neural substrates of this transition, the authors used chemogenetic inhibition (hM4Di + CNO) to selectively silence the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) or the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) during either phase, and conducted reward-devaluation tests at both time points.

The inhibition pattern was doubly dissociated across phases. During learning, silencing the mPFC abolished task acquisition, whereas silencing the DLS had no effect; during maintenance, the dependencies reversed, with mPFC inhibition leaving performance intact and DLS inhibition no longer impairing accuracy. The decisive result, however, was what silencing the DLS *did* to behaviour during maintenance: rather than abolishing performance, it restored devaluation-sensitivity, returning the mice to a goal-directed strategy. Silencing both mPFC and DLS together collapsed performance entirely, demonstrating that the mPFC solution had remained functional throughout overtraining and was suppressed by, but not eliminated by, the DLS takeover.

This pattern is incompatible with a migration account. If the cortical solution were supplanted, silencing the DLS in an overtrained animal should not instantly recover a flexible, devaluation-sensitive strategy. The reversibility of the handoff implies instead that the cortical solution is retained intact throughout overtraining, and that something *gates which system is expressed* rather than transferring a solution from one region to another. The mechanistic puzzle is therefore the following: what allows a learned cortical controller to be switched off as a habit takes over, yet switched back on instantly when the habitual controller is removed? A satisfying answer must preserve the cortical weights, suppress their expression during habitual control, and restore that expression rapidly — and it must do so as a consequence of learning, since the animal is never told when to hand control over.

## 1.3 Dopamine as a mechanism for gating control

We argue that dopamine is well suited to this gating role, for two reasons that jointly motivate the model developed here.

The classical view treats phasic dopamine as a global reward prediction error that modifies synaptic weights (Schultz, 2016), yet the empirical evidence demands a substantially richer picture. Pharmacological dissections in humans confirm that dopamine enhancement simultaneously boosts fast working-memory deployment *and* slow reinforcement learning, while independently blunting sensitivity to effort costs — dissociable effects that track individual differences in striatal dopamine synthesis capacity rather than any single teaching function (Westbrook et al., 2025). This multiplicity motivates the two-component architecture developed here.

First, dopamine supports two *independent* learning signals, only one of which carries reward. Beyond its classical reward prediction error role, movement-related dopamine in the tail of the striatum encodes an *action prediction error* (APE) — a mismatch between the expected and the executed action — that reinforces stimulus–action associations without representing reward at all (Greenstreet et al., 2025). Reward and action prediction errors operate in parallel across different striatal territories, allowing value-based and value-free learning to proceed simultaneously. This parallelism has a natural substrate in the opponent architecture of the basal ganglia: Frank (2025) shows that D1-receptor-bearing neurons accumulate evidence for actions driven by dopamine bursts, while D2-receptor-bearing neurons accumulate evidence against actions driven by dopamine dips — a cost-coding signal that is, by construction, insensitive to the magnitude of reward. A habitual controller implemented through this D2-like, cost-avoiding pathway is therefore *structurally* devaluation-insensitive: it never represented the value of outcomes, and cannot revise its behaviour when that value changes. This furnishes a mechanistic, rather than merely descriptive, account of the defining property of habits.

Second, dopamine plays a dual role in recurrent neural circuits: it *builds* latent behavioural attractors through long-term synaptic plasticity, and independently *reveals* them through rapid neuromodulation of neural excitability (Naudé et al., 2024). These two roles are dissociable: plasticity-alone produces only shallow attractor basins that do not reliably drive approach behaviour; excitability-modulation-alone has no directional effect without a pre-existing attractor; only their combination produces the deep, wide basins that generate reliable goal-directed action. This architecture implies that a learned solution can remain *latent* — its weights intact but its expression suppressed — until dopamine's excitability action reveals it. The instant reactivation observed by Villet et al. is precisely what this mechanism predicts: the cortical solution was not erased during overtraining, but held in a low-expression state whose reinstatement requires no learning, only a rise in dopaminergic drive. The framing of dopamine as a multiplicative expression gain has independent computational support: Lloyd & Dayan (2015) show that phasic dopamine transients act as a gain on decision accumulation, generating the empirically observed ramps before goal-directed action as a natural consequence of threshold-crossing statistics — a result that grounds the present model's `r_out = gain(DA) · tanh(h)` formulation in a principled decision-theoretic account. More broadly, Lloyd & Dayan (2023) recast dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens as an *instrument of cognitive control* actively regulated by the prefrontal cortex to resolve action conflicts, rather than a passive reporter of learned values — a framing that motivates the present model's DA-request neuron as a learned, cost-minimised control parameter rather than a fixed signal.

Within the goal-directed system specifically, dopamine acts on two functionally distinct timescales. Receptor-level evidence establishes that D1 and D2 stimulation in prefrontal cortex drive opposing network dynamics, placing circuits in one of two bistable states with characteristically different stability properties (Seamans & Yang, 2004). The relationship between dopamine level and working-memory performance follows an inverted-U: too little dopamine destabilises representations; too much rigidifies them; optimal performance requires a tuned operating point that shifts with task demands and individual baseline dopamine tone (Cools & D'Esposito, 2011; Cools, 2019). Cools (2019) further proposes that prefrontal circuits actively regulate their own dopaminergic tone in response to environmental demands — an emergent switching principle consistent with a handoff that is learned rather than scheduled. The precise direction of D1 and D2 contributions remains under investigation: Kutter et al. (2026) showed, using focal microiontophoretic application in macaque prefrontal cortex, that D1 receptor stimulation promotes *transient*, dynamically-evolving decision codes suited to moment-to-moment updating, while D2 receptor stimulation promotes *persistent*, attractor-like codes stable across the delay — findings that may not map cleanly onto earlier receptor-affinity accounts, and which underscore that receptor function is context-dependent. Naudé et al. (2024) offer a functional characterisation agnostic to receptor direction: fast dopaminergic effects **widen** attractor basins, accelerating convergence to a decision state, while slow effects **deepen** and stabilise those basins, supporting maintenance across delays. The present model implements this widen/deepen distinction as two interleaved subpopulations within the goal-directed recurrent network, with time constants tuned to the respective timescales.

Taken together, these properties suggest a single mechanism for the Villet phenomenon. If (i) the habitual system is trained by action prediction error and never by reward, making its devaluation-insensitivity structural, and (ii) dopamine sets a multiplicative gain on the *expression* of the goal-directed system's intact learned activations — and if this gain falls as the habitual system becomes competent — then a network should reproduce both the goal-directed-to-habitual transition and its instant reversibility. The dormant cortical solution is one whose expression has been turned down, not erased, and which a rise in dopaminergic drive can bring back without relearning. Neural variability in the mPFC may be a further constituent of this mechanism: Findling et al. (2025) show that mPFC fluctuations proportional to belief-update magnitude implement near-optimal adaptive behaviour without any explicit uncertainty inference, suggesting that dopamine-modulated stochasticity in the goal-directed network may actively shape the timing of control transfer rather than merely permitting it.

The extension of this framework to the full dopaminergic architecture of the basal ganglia — including the opponent direct and indirect pathways, whose nonlinear Hebbian plasticity confers normative reinforcement-learning advantages in sparse-reward environments (Jaskir & Frank), the wave-like spatiotemporal dynamics of dopamine release across dorsomedial and dorsolateral striatum (Hamid et al., 2021), and the anatomically segregated VTA and substantia nigra compacta pathways that drive functionally distinct striatal signals during learning (Engel et al., 2024) — constitutes a natural next step and is developed in the Discussion as future work.

## 1.4 The present study

We test this proposal in a minimal, biologically constrained model: a two-area recurrent network performing the same DNMTP T-maze, in which a goal-directed system (mPFC/DMS analogue) and a habitual system (DLS analogue) compete for control under dopaminergic gating. Rather than committing to a fixed architecture, we ask whether four specific design choices are jointly sufficient to reproduce the Villet pattern, and test which are individually necessary through targeted ablations.

The four choices are the following. (1) *An allocentric/egocentric observational split*: the goal-directed system receives the full spatial map of the maze (allocentric, model-based), whereas the habitual system receives only the phase signals with position withheld (egocentric, model-free) — making the systems functionally distinct with respect to planning. (2) *A low-rank recurrent structure in the habitual system*: the habitual network's recurrent weight matrix is constrained to low rank, making the claim that habitual motor sequences are low-dimensional structural rather than incidental (Echeveste et al., 2020). (3) *A value-free action-prediction-error learning rule*: the habitual system is trained by APE and never by reward (Greenstreet et al., 2025), making its devaluation-insensitivity a consequence of the learning rule rather than a tuning choice. (4) *Dopamine-gated expression-level gain on the goal-directed system*: dopamine multiplicatively scales the output activations of the goal-directed system, `r_out = gain(DA) · tanh(h)`, modulating how strongly intact learned activations are expressed without altering the weights themselves — the property that makes instant fallback possible (Naudé et al., 2024).

Control transfers because the goal-directed system is under continual pressure to minimise its own dopamine demand and can do so without sacrificing performance only once the habitual system carries the policy — so that no rule schedules the transition. This emergent handoff is subjected to an explicit falsification test: silencing the habitual system after overtraining should cause the goal-directed system's dopamine demand to rise back toward its learning-phase level, confirming that the handoff was locked to habitual competence rather than to training time.

Our objectives are accordingly to determine whether such a network (a) learns the task; (b) shows an emergent, competence-locked transfer of control from the goal-directed to the habitual system; (c) exhibits Villet's devaluation dissociation and lesion-by-phase pattern; and (d) reproduces the reactivation result, the signature of a genuinely reversible handoff. We further ask whether the habitual system maintains a readable working-memory representation of which arm was visited across the delay — a prerequisite for correct choice performance that the DLS must satisfy at maintenance. For each of these objectives, we specify in advance whether a null result will be reported as a positive falsification (H5) or as a negative finding, and we commit to reporting all results without qualification.

The contribution of this work is a mechanistic, computationally explicit account of a phenomenon that the dominant migration view does not naturally explain — the *reversibility* of cortico-striatal control transfer. By locating the transition in the dopaminergic expression of intact weights, the habit's value-insensitivity in a value-free learning rule, and the system's low-dimensional stereotypy in a rank constraint, the model treats the goal-directed-to-habitual shift and its instant reversal as two faces of a single dopamine mechanism. Should it succeed, it yields directly testable biological predictions: most pointedly, that disrupting prefrontal dopamine during maintenance should restore devaluation-sensitivity, and that the timing of the behavioural transition should track the DLS's learning speed rather than training duration.
