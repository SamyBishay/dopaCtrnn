Computational neuroscience admits a spectrum of modelling philosophies, and a model's epistemic value depends as much on where it sits on that spectrum as on whether it fits the data. At one pole lies **biophysical realism**: conductance-based, multi-compartment models in the Hodgkin–Huxley tradition that aim for mechanistic fidelity to the underlying biology, exemplified for this problem by the MAGNet model of Naudé et al. (2024), with its leaky integrate-and-fire dynamics and explicit NMDA-receptor potentiation. Such models maximise biological interpretability but pay for it in tractability and in an abundance of free parameters that are difficult to constrain from behaviour alone; their richness can make it hard to identify _which_ features are doing the explanatory work. At the opposite pole lie **normative** accounts, which ask not how the brain implements a computation but what problem the computation optimally solves — the dual-process literature's recent turn, in which MDL-C (Moskovitz et al., 2024) derives a controller–default architecture from the minimum-description-length principle and the demands of generalisation, with no reference to neuroanatomy. Between these poles sit **connectionist** or **canonical-circuit** models — rate-based recurrent networks (CTRNNs) of the kind used here — which trade biophysical detail for analytic and dynamical tractability while retaining enough structure (recurrence, low-dimensional population dynamics, attractor states) to be related to neural recordings. This intermediate level is where much of systems-level theory now operates, because it is dimensional enough to be mechanistic yet abstract enough that one can reason about _why_ a network behaves as it does.

This project began nearer the realist pole and moved deliberately toward a different methodological commitment. The initial impulse was toward **biological plausibility for its own sake** — accumulating mechanisms (a dopamine-modulated expression gain, a D1/D2 receptor-affinity timescale split, opponent Go/NoGo readouts, an allocentric/egocentric observation asymmetry, separate action- and reward-prediction-error learning rules) on the implicit assumption that more biological detail yields a better model. The decisive methodological shift was the recognition that a model carrying many coupled mechanisms can _fit_ a phenomenon without _explaining_ it: faced with such a model, one cannot say which mechanism is load-bearing, and a model built to reproduce a result will reproduce it almost regardless of whether its mechanisms are the right ones. The remedy is the **necessary-and-sufficiency** discipline that organises the present work. Rather than demonstrating _existence_ — that one richly-specified model reproduces the target phenomena — the experimental ladder establishes, by **ablation** and **minimal-pair** contrasts, _which_ mechanisms are individually necessary (their removal abolishes the phenomenon) and what the _minimal sufficient_ architecture is (the smallest model in which the phenomenon first appears). Each mechanism that survived from the realist phase did so because it earned its place in an ablation, not because it was biologically appealing; mechanisms that could not be shown to be load-bearing — the receptor-affinity split, the opponent readouts — were demoted to deliberate modelling idealisations or to future work rather than asserted. The model thus occupies the connectionist middle ground by design, biologically grounded enough to map onto the cortico-striatal substrate yet minimal enough that every retained component is a tested claim. The journey was, in short, from _plausibility_ to _parsimony_: from asking "is this biologically realistic?" to asking "is this necessary, and is it sufficient?" — the latter being the question that distinguishes a mechanistic explanation from a mere fit.
