## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Two systems for instrumental control, and the criterion that separates them

Adaptive behaviour is widely held to arise from at least two dissociable systems for instrumental control. A **goal-directed** system selects actions by representing their consequences and the current value of those consequences; it is flexible and sensitive to changes in the action–outcome contingency and to outcome devaluation. A **habitual** system selects actions through learned stimulus–response associations; it is fast and automatic but inflexible and, by its operational definition, insensitive to outcome devaluation (Adams & Dickinson, 1981 **[ADD]**; Dickinson, 1985 **[ADD]**). The same division is captured computationally by the distinction between model-based control, which plans over an internal model of the environment, and model-free control, which caches action values from experience (Gläscher et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2014). The systems are at least partly dissociable in the brain: prefrontal and dorsomedial circuits support flexible, goal-directed control; dorsolateral striatal circuits support habitual control; and individual differences in prefrontal versus striatal dopamine track reliance on the two strategies (Doll et al., 2016).

The empirical criterion that distinguishes the two is the **reinforcer-devaluation test** (Adams & Dickinson, 1981 **[ADD]**; Balleine & Dickinson, 1998 **[ADD]**): if devaluing the outcome suppresses the response, the response was goal-directed; if the response persists unchanged, it was habitual. A central, well-replicated observation is that control **shifts** from the goal-directed to the habitual system as a behaviour is repeated: with overtraining, an action that was initially devaluation-sensitive becomes automatic and devaluation-insensitive. Recording across cortex and striatum during this transition, Smith and Graybiel (2013) found that habit formation is accompanied by task-bracketing activity in both the infralimbic cortex and the dorsolateral striatum — the two regions operating as coordinated "dual operators" rather than the striatum acting alone.

### 1.2 From information migration to a reversible handoff: the puzzle posed by Villet et al. (2025)

The classical interpretation of this shift is one of **information migration**, or consolidation: as a habit forms, the locus of control — and, on stronger readings, the underlying memory itself — moves from cortical to striatal circuits, and the cortical contribution is progressively supplanted. On this view, once a behaviour is habitual the cortical solution is no longer needed and, in the limit, no longer available.

Villet et al. (2025) provide a direct challenge to that prediction. Using a delayed non-match-to-place (DNMP) T-maze in mice, with chemogenetic inhibition of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) across a **learning** and a **maintenance** phase, they characterised the transition through a sequence of informative manipulations. During early learning, behaviour was goal-directed and devaluation-sensitive and depended on the mPFC: silencing the mPFC prevented learning, whereas silencing the DLS did not. After overtraining, the same behaviour was automated and devaluation-insensitive, and the dependencies reversed — DLS rather than mPFC now mattered. The decisive result, however, was what silencing the DLS *did* during maintenance: rather than abolishing performance, it **re-engaged the goal-directed strategy**, devaluation-sensitivity included; and silencing the mPFC and DLS *together* collapsed performance entirely. The cortical solution had therefore not been deleted or overwritten during habit formation. The authors conclude that the memory trace "remains in a **dormant state** — less energy-consuming for the system — while allowing for rapid flexibility in case of task modification," explicitly challenging the migration view.

This pattern is difficult to reconcile with information migration. If the cortical solution were supplanted, silencing the striatum in an overtrained animal should not recover a devaluation-sensitive, goal-directed strategy on demand. The **reversibility** of the handoff implies instead that the cortical solution is retained intact throughout overtraining, and that something *gates which system is expressed* rather than transferring a solution from one region to another. The mechanistic puzzle is therefore: what allows a learned cortical controller to be switched off as a habit takes over, yet switched back on rapidly when the habitual controller is removed? A satisfying answer must **preserve** the cortical weights, **suppress** their expression during habitual control, and **restore** that expression quickly — and it must do so as a *consequence of learning*, since the animal is never told when to hand control over. Villet et al. establish *that* this occurs; they do not provide the *mechanism*. That gap is what this work addresses.

### 1.3 Cortical dopamine as a mechanism for gating expression

We propose that **cortical dopamine** is well suited to this gating role, and that the reversible handoff is, mechanistically, a story about the dopaminergic control of *expression* rather than the transfer of a solution. The proposal rests on two complementary lines of evidence and one structural argument.

**Theoretically**, Naudé et al. (2024) provide the dynamical principle the puzzle requires. In their MAGNet model — a recurrent network embodied through an action–perception loop with the task space — dopamine plays a dual role that they dissociate explicitly. Dopamine-dependent **plasticity builds latent attractors**: goal-encoding assemblies that attract network dynamics, but only weakly and locally. Dopamine-dependent modulation of **excitability reveals** these attractors, by multiplicatively widening and deepening their basins so that the encoded goal becomes accessible globally, from distal states. Crucially, MAGNet **decouples learning from performance**: a plasticity-built attractor "does not necessarily affect neural dynamics" until excitability reveals it — their illustration is latent learning, where a sated animal learns a maze without changing its behaviour and expresses that learning only upon deprivation. This is, in dynamical form, precisely the dormant-trace concept Villet's reactivation result demands: a learned solution, built and retained, whose *expression* is gated by a fast neuromodulatory signal.

**Empirically**, the cortical specificity is grounded in Kutter et al. (2026), who showed in primate dorsolateral prefrontal cortex that D1 and D2 receptors differentially control the **strength** and **temporal stability** of abstract decision codes — establishing that cortical dopamine causally tunes *how strongly* and *how persistently* a representation is held. The relevant dopamine for our model is thus cortical and modulatory: it sets the gain on a prefrontal working-memory representation, not a reward-prediction error in the striatum.

We formalise this as a **multiplicative expression gain on intact cortical weights**, $W_{\text{eff}} = f(\text{DA}) \cdot W$. Dopamine does not change *what* the cortical network has learned, only *how strongly* that learning is expressed. This is the discrete-network analogue of Naudé's excitability-reveals-plasticity mechanism, and it is exactly the operation Villet's result demands — a way to suppress a cortical solution's expression during habitual control and restore it rapidly without erasing it.

The habitual system, by contrast, learns by a **value-free action prediction error**: an abstraction of the dopaminergic action-prediction-error (APE) signal that Greenstreet et al. (2025) identified in the tail of the striatum, which reinforces stimulus–action associations without representing reward. (This is the single striatal-dopamine commitment in the model, and it functions only to justify the habitual learning rule.) Because the habitual system never represents reward, its behaviour is **devaluation-insensitive by construction** — furnishing a mechanistic, rather than merely descriptive, account of the defining property of habits.

Finally, a **structural argument** licenses giving the cortical network two timescales. Naudé frames dopamine's role in cognitive motivation as a "double duty": **widening** basins of attraction to set a decision, and **deepening** them to stabilise a representation in working memory. We endow the goal-directed network with fast units (decision: basin-widening) and slow units (maintenance: basin-deepening), which lets a single expression-gain mechanism serve both the rapid action selection and the delay-period maintenance the task requires.[^d1d2]

[^d1d2]: We frame the two timescales in Naudé's widen-versus-deepen terms rather than committing to a fixed D1/D2 receptor mapping, because the directionality is contested: the classical dual-state account (D1 stabilises, D2 destabilises) and Kutter et al.'s (2026) finding for abstract decision codes (which they report as *diverging* from classical predictions) point in opposite directions for exactly the maintenance regime we model. The widen/deepen framing is the one our dynamics actually implement and is agnostic to that dispute.

### 1.4 The present study and its hypotheses

We test this proposal in a minimal, biologically constrained model: a two-area recurrent network performing the same DNMP T-maze, in which a goal-directed system (mPFC/DMS) and a habitual system (DLS) compete for control under cortical dopaminergic gating. Critically, we do **not** seek merely to show that one rich model reproduces Villet's data — existence is cheap, and a model built to fit will fit. We seek the **simplest necessary and sufficient** conditions for the phenomena, established by an experimental *ladder* in which each mechanism is isolated by a minimal pair: removed or varied, one at a time, to test whether the target phenomenon survives (see §2.5).

The model commits to three properties drawn from the argument above. First, **expression-level gain**: dopamine multiplicatively scales the expression of the goal-directed system's intact weights, modulating how strongly the learned representation is expressed rather than altering the weights themselves — the property that makes instant reversibility possible. Second, a **value-free habitual learner**: trained by an action-prediction-error-like signal and never by reward, which makes its devaluation-insensitivity structural. Third, an **emergent handoff**: control transfers because the goal-directed system is under continual pressure to minimise its own dopaminergic demand and can do so safely only once the habitual system carries the policy — so that no rule schedules the transition. Two asymmetries make the systems functionally distinct: the goal-directed system receives the full observation including spatial coordinates (affording location-based planning), whereas the habitual system receives a reduced, position-free observation and is therefore obliged to a reactive, stimulus–response policy; and the goal-directed network carries the two-timescale structure that supports delay-period maintenance.

From this we derive three theoretical hypotheses, operationalised in the Method:

1. **The handoff is emergent, not scheduled.** Control should transfer to the habitual system as, and only as, that system becomes competent, so that the timing of the transfer tracks each network's learning speed rather than a fixed point in training.
2. **The two systems are differentially sensitive to value.** Devaluation should impair goal-directed control but leave habitual control intact, by construction of the value-free learning rule — reproducing Villet's devaluation dissociation.
3. **The handoff is reversible.** Silencing the habitual system after overtraining should preserve performance, raise the goal-directed system's dopaminergic demand, and restore devaluation-sensitivity, as the dormant cortical solution re-expresses.

The third hypothesis is also the model's principal point of **falsification**. Because the dopaminergic signal that drives expression is, in the model, shaped during reward-based learning, an apparent reactivation could in principle be a trivial consequence of the control loop rather than evidence of a preserved cortical solution. We therefore subject any reactivation result to a stringent control (§2.6): if recovery is attributable only to the gating variable moving — and not to the preserved goal-directed policy doing the task — then the handoff was a schedule in disguise, and we commit in advance to reporting such an outcome as a negative result.

The contribution of this work is to offer a mechanistic, reproducible account of the one feature the dominant migration view does not naturally explain — the **reversibility** of cortico-striatal control transfer — and to establish, by ablation rather than assertion, which mechanisms are necessary for it.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Overview

The model comprises two areas, each a continuous-time recurrent neural network (CTRNN), coupled by a policy-mixing process. A **goal-directed** network (mPFC/DMS) is trained by an actor–critic algorithm on task reward; cortical dopamine sets a multiplicative gain on the expression of its recurrent weights. A **habitual** network (DLS) is trained by a value-free action-prediction-error signal plus an intrinsic efficiency term, and never sees reward. Control is allocated between the two by a mixing weight that depends on the goal-directed system's dopaminergic demand. The model is trained on a free-navigation DNMP T-maze and then subjected to an experimental ladder (§2.5) and a battery of analyses (§2.6) that mirror the chemogenetic and devaluation manipulations of Villet et al. (2025).

Two functions of the dopaminergic signal are conceptually distinct and the ladder is designed to dissociate them: an **expression-gain** function (how strongly the cortical representation is expressed, $W_{\text{eff}} = f(\text{DA}) \cdot W$) and an **arbitration** function (which system drives behaviour). Separating these is the enabling step for the necessity test in §2.5.

### 2.2 Task: free-navigation DNMP T-maze

The task follows the structure of Villet et al. (2025): delayed non-match-to-place. Unlike the head-fixed or clamped-input working-memory paradigms common in modelling, the agent **navigates freely** throughout all phases, so that the sampled arm's identity must be held in recurrent state rather than read from a held input — an embodiment choice motivated by Naudé et al.'s (2024) argument that an action–perception loop differs fundamentally from an input–output mapping. A trial proceeds through four phases: **pre-sample** (navigate from the start to the junction, triggering the sample); **sample** (one arm is blocked; navigate to the open arm's end); **delay** (free movement while the phase signal decays exponentially, so arm identity is no longer observable); and **choice** (navigate to the *non-matching* — previously blocked — arm, which is rewarded).

**Parametric difficulty.** The maze is a parametric grid following the reference implementation: an odd edge length sets the corridor width, and a difficulty parameter sets the **stem length** — the distance, and hence the time, between sampling and choosing. A longer stem raises the working-memory and credit-assignment load without altering the rule or the arms; the delay proper is additionally controlled by a counter under a curriculum (§2.4). This dissociates two contributions to task difficulty — navigational distance and explicit delay — that are conflated in fixed-geometry designs.

**Observations.** The two systems receive different observations, operationalising their functional asymmetry. The goal-directed system receives the agent's normalised position together with the phase signals; the habitual system receives the phase signals only, **without position**, constraining it to a reactive policy that cannot spatially plan the non-match rule.

**Actions and reward.** The action set is {North, South, East, West, Wait}. Task reward combines a terminal reward for the correct non-match choice, dense shaping bonuses for reaching the junction and arm ends, and a small per-step cost favouring efficiency. The habitual system is trained on a separate **intrinsic** reward stream (efficiency cost plus a completion bonus) and never on the food reward, making its policy value-independent by construction.

### 2.3 Network architecture

Each network is a CTRNN with learned, two-group time constants (fast units for decision, slow units for maintenance), following the discretised dynamics $h \leftarrow h + (\Delta t/\tau)(-h + r W^{\top} + x W_{\text{in}}^{\top} + b)$ with $r = \tanh(h)$.

The **goal-directed network** computes, in addition to action logits and a state value, a scalar dopaminergic demand. This signal sets a multiplicative **expression gain** on the network's output activation — the discrete analogue of Naudé et al.'s (2024) excitability potentiation acting on a plasticity-built assembly. Fast units are modulated phasically and slow units through a low-pass (tonic) integrator, implementing the widen-to-decide / deepen-to-maintain duality. The expression gain leaves the learned weights unchanged and modulates only how strongly they drive behaviour, which is the property that permits instant reversibility.

The **habitual network** is a CTRNN with opponent Go/NoGo readouts (policy = Go − NoGo). It may be parameterised at full rank (default) or **low rank** ($W = (m n^{\top})/N$, rank tunable), the latter making the hypothesis of a low-dimensional habit *structural* rather than a post-hoc finding and connecting to the analytic tradition for low-rank recurrent networks (Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic, 2018 **[ADD]**; Dubreuil et al., 2022 **[ADD]**).

**Policy mixing.** The combined policy is a convex mixture of the two systems' policies, weighted by an arbitration term that depends on the goal-directed dopaminergic demand and on a motivational factor (set to zero to simulate devaluation). In the current implementation the expression-gain and arbitration functions are driven by a common dopaminergic variable; the ladder (§2.5) separates them in order to test the necessity of expression-gating independently of arbitration.

### 2.4 Training

The two systems are optimised by separate Adam optimisers. The goal-directed system is trained by **advantage actor–critic** on task reward, with generalised advantage estimation and running-window return normalisation to reduce variance. The habitual system is trained by a combination of a value-free **action-prediction-error** term (cross-entropy against the executed action) and an intrinsic-efficiency term; it receives no reward gradient.

The **emergent handoff** is driven by a quadratic penalty on the goal-directed dopaminergic demand, introduced after a warm-up and ramped to full strength. This pressure pushes the goal-directed system to minimise its own dopaminergic expression, which it can do without losing reward only once the habitual system has become competent enough to carry the policy — so that control transfers as a consequence of learning rather than on a fixed schedule. A **delay curriculum** lengthens the maintenance interval as combined and habitual performance pass threshold, and checkpoints corresponding to Villet's **learning** and **maintenance** phases are saved by accuracy criteria (learning: combined system competent but habitual-alone not yet; maintenance: habitual-alone carrying performance). Training is vectorised over parallel environments and is fully checkpoint-resumable.

### 2.5 The experimental ladder

The methodological core of this work is a **ladder of minimal models**, designed to establish *necessity and sufficiency* rather than mere existence. Each rung contrasts two variants differing in exactly one mechanism — implemented as a configuration flag so that both arms run from identical code — and asks whether a target phenomenon survives when that mechanism is removed or altered. Two phenomena are targeted: the **handoff** (and its reversibility) and the **devaluation dissociation**.

- **Rung 0 — learning gate.** A precondition, not a contrast: the base model must solve the task above chance (accuracy ≥ 0.80, binomial *p* < 10⁻³, ≥ 2 seeds) before any analysis is interpretable. An attractor decoder, a handoff metric, or a devaluation drop computed on a model at chance is an artifact, and no rung above runs until this gate is met.
- **Rung 1 — is a second system necessary?** Single CTRNN versus the two-system model. Prediction: the single network learns the task but shows no handoff (nothing to hand off to).
- **Rung 2 — does reversibility need expression-gating?** *(the crux)* A scheduled/fixed gate (arbitration only) versus dopaminergic expression-gating. Both may produce a handoff; only the expression-gating variant should produce **reactivation** when the habit is silenced. This rung requires separating the expression-gain and arbitration signals, since reversibility cannot be removed independently of arbitration while they share a variable.
- **Rung 3 — does reversibility need *multiplicative* gain?** Multiplicative versus additive gating, testing whether the specific form (Naudé's NMDA-potentiation analogue) is necessary.
- **Rung 4 — the minimal habit.** A sweep over habitual-network rank, identifying the minimum dimensionality that supports the handoff.
- **Rung 5 — the devaluation dissociation.** Value-free versus value-coupled habitual learning, testing whether the value-free rule is necessary and sufficient for learning-phase devaluation-sensitivity with maintenance-phase insensitivity.

The deliverable is a table — one row per rung — of which mechanisms, when removed, abolish each phenomenon (necessity) and which is the smallest model in which each phenomenon first appears (sufficiency). This table *is* the scientific argument.

### 2.6 Analyses

All analyses are performed without gradient on frozen weights, at a significance threshold of α = 0.05 unless noted; the learning test uses an exact binomial test against chance at *p* < 10⁻³. The operational hypotheses map onto Villet's manipulations:

- **Learning (H1).** Accuracy with an exact binomial test against chance.
- **Emergent handoff (H2).** The time course of habitual-alone accuracy, combined accuracy, and the arbitration weight; the onset at which the habitual system becomes competent should *precede* the decline in goal-directed expression. To guard against the handoff locking to the penalty schedule rather than to competence, the penalty timetable is varied across conditions and onset is tested against competence with schedule timing partialled out.
- **Devaluation dissociation (H3).** Accuracy under intact versus zeroed motivation, at the learning and maintenance checkpoints; a large drop in learning and a negligible drop in maintenance constitutes the dissociation (the operational devaluation criterion of Adams & Dickinson, 1981 **[ADD]**).
- **Necessity by phase (H4).** Accuracy under silencing of each system at each phase, replicating Villet's lesion-by-phase pattern. We note that inference-time silencing of a *trained* network indexes what the network currently relies on, not what was necessary to *develop* the phenomenon; developmental necessity is what the ladder (§2.5) establishes.
- **Reactivation (H5)** *(the crux, with mandatory control).* Following silencing of the habitual system at the maintenance checkpoint, we measure whether goal-directed performance and devaluation-sensitivity recover. The result is admitted only if it survives a **gate-clamp control**: the arbitration weight is clamped open and we test whether the *preserved goal-directed policy* can perform the task. If recovery is attributable only to the gating variable moving, the reactivation is a control-loop artifact ("a schedule in disguise") and is reported as a negative result.
- **Working-memory attractor (H6).** Delay-period network states are analysed for separability by sampled-arm identity. Beyond linear decodability — which a feedforward map of a separable input would also satisfy — we test the *dynamical* claim: that delay states relax onto fixed points whose basin geometry is modulated by the expression gain, following the latent-attractor framework of Naudé et al. (2024). For the low-rank habitual network this is examined in connectivity (rather than data-driven) coordinates.
- **Negative control (H7).** All measures on an untrained network at the same delay.

A discipline applies throughout: hyperparameters are tuned only to achieve *learning* (Rung 0); they are never tuned to make a hypothesis pass. A hypothesis that fails on a network that genuinely learned is a scientific result, reported as such.

## 3. Future Research

### 3.1 The model's distinctive prediction: handoff timing is controllable

The hypotheses above reproduce and mechanistically explain Villet et al. (2025); the model's value to experimentalists, however, lies in a prediction Villet did not test and which **distinguishes the expression-gating account from migration**. Villet used a *fixed* 90-second delay throughout and explicitly noted that this fixed interval likely reinforced the DLS's task-bracketing role — but they did not manipulate it. In the present model, delay length is an explicit parameter, and because the goal-directed system is needed precisely when the working-memory demand is high, the model predicts that **delay length controls the rate of the handoff**: lengthening the delay should *delay or prevent* the transition to habitual control (keeping behaviour mPFC-dependent and devaluation-sensitive), whereas shortening it should *accelerate* the transition. This opposes the migration account, under which handoff timing depends on cumulative training alone and not on the moment-to-moment demand. The prediction is directly testable in Villet's apparatus by varying the sample-to-choice interval across cohorts.

### 3.2 Graded, history-dependent reactivation, and a falsifiable fork

A second prediction sharpens the reactivation result. The model carries a continuous index of goal-directed expression that Villet's criterion-based design lacks, and predicts that the *magnitude* of goal-directed reactivation after DLS silencing should be **graded** — predictable from the residual goal-directed expression at the moment of silencing — rather than all-or-none. This yields a clean falsifiable fork between accounts of the cortical trace. Because the expression-gating account holds the cortical weights intact, it predicts that reactivation magnitude is **constant** with respect to time-since-handoff; a genuine-degradation account (a weak form of migration, in which the cortical trace slowly decays once supplanted) predicts that reactivation **declines** the longer the habit has been in control. Measuring reactivation magnitude as a function of overtraining duration would adjudicate between them — and a behavioural proxy for handoff completeness (such as response time, which shortens as a rule is proceduralised) could stand in for the internal variable in vivo.

### 3.3 Separating the dopaminergic signals, and a biophysical bridge

The present model couples the expression-gain and arbitration functions through a single dopaminergic variable; the ladder (§2.5) dissociates them experimentally, but a natural refinement is to instantiate them as **two signals** with distinct sources — an expression-gain signal tied to the cortical working-memory code (the Kutter/Naudé mechanism) and an arbitration signal that need not be dopaminergic at all. This would let the cortical-dopamine claim stand on its own and remove the principal interpretive ambiguity in the reactivation result. A further step toward biological realism would replace the rate-based CTRNN with a spiking, biophysically detailed implementation in the spirit of Naudé et al.'s (2024) MAGNet, in which the expression gain is NMDA-receptor potentiation acting on a plasticity-built assembly — making the abstraction in $W_{\text{eff}} = f(\text{DA}) \cdot W$ mechanistically explicit and connecting the model to the cellular dopamine literature (Seamans & Yang, 2004; Cools & D'Esposito, 2011 **[ADD]**).

### 3.4 What drives the handoff: striatal agency dopamine as a sequel

The present model treats the *timing* of the handoff as emerging from a penalty on cortical dopaminergic demand — a deliberately minimal mechanism. A richer and biologically grounded alternative would make the handoff *driven* by a striatal signal of agency. Hamid et al. (2021) showed that dorsomedial striatal dopamine ramps encode evidence for instrumental control and predict trial-by-trial behavioural adjustment, with wave-like dopamine propagating between striatal subregions as a function of task demands. A model in which an agency signal of this kind governs when control is ceded to the habitual system — rather than a fixed penalty schedule — is a substantial architectural extension and a clear sequel to the present work. We scope it out here deliberately: the present model is cortical-dopamine-centric and concerns the *expression* of the cortical trace, which is the half of the circuit Villet's reactivation result speaks to; the striatal-agency mechanism concerns the *control-allocation* half and is a distinct question. (We are careful not to overstate the Hamid result: the dorsomedial signal encodes evidence for the animal's instrumental control over outcomes, not a representation of which striatal subregion is currently driving behaviour.)

### 3.5 The low-dimensional habit and its dynamics

The low-rank parameterisation of the habitual network invites a reduction in the style of the low-rank recurrent-network literature (Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic, 2018 **[ADD]**; Dubreuil et al., 2022 **[ADD]**): identifying the minimum rank that supports the habitual policy, and characterising the delay-period dynamics in connectivity coordinates — projecting onto the structure vectors that define the low-rank connectivity, locating fixed points in that reduced space, and reporting the overlaps that, in mean-field theory, determine the dynamics. This would turn the qualitative claim that "the habit is low-dimensional" into a quantitative, mechanistically interpretable result, and would let the working-memory representation be analysed as a behavioural-potential-energy landscape (Naudé et al., 2024) whose basin geometry the expression gain controls.
