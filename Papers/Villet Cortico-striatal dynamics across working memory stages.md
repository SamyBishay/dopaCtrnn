
# Villet Cortico-striatal dynamics across working memory stages
Maxime Villet, Benjamin Azoulay, Jacques Barik, Hélène Marie, and Ingrid Bethus
 
Institut de Pharmacologie Moléculaire et Cellulaire, Université Côte d'Azur, CNRS UMR7275 - INSERM U1323, 660 Route des Lucioles, Sophia-Antipolis, Valbonne 06560, Alpes Maritimes, France
 
Advance access publication date: 17 December 2025
 
---
 
Working memory depends on the temporary retention and manipulation of information, bridging the gap between short-term memory and information processing functions. When the same working memory task is repeated over several days, it raises the question of whether the rule or task set becomes automated (or proceduralized). The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is crucial for working memory. Yet, the role of the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) in the automation (proceduralization) of rules or task sets remains to be clarified.
 
Using a longitudinal approach of the "delay non-match to place" (DNMP) task in a T-maze combined with chemogenetic inhibition of the mPFC or DLS in mice, we show that the mPFC becomes less critical in the maintenance phase of the task as behavior progressively shifts towards automation. During this phase, the DLS facilitates automated processing. Silencing through chemogenetic inhibition of the DLS during maintenance triggers an adaptation in learning strategies, reactivating a goal-directed behavior. Our findings strengthen memory traces as a dynamic reorganization of neural networks, challenging the classical view of information migration between brain structures. We propose that the memory trace remains in a dormant state—less energy-consuming for the system—while allowing for rapid flexibility in case of task modification.
 
Keywords: automated behavior; dorsolateral striatum; goal directed behavior; medial prefrontal cortex; working memory.

## Introduction
 
Working memory refers to the process of temporarily holding new incoming information, while simultaneously performing cognitive manipulation on this information for a short duration (Eichenbaum 2002). Alan Baddeley and his colleagues first recognized the importance of distinguishing the cognitive and storage processes in short-term memory, and replaced the concept of a unitary short-term memory with a multiple components conception of working memory that mediates both storage and central executive functions (Baddeley and Hitch 1974; Baddeley 2000, 1986).
 
However, in this multiple component's conception of the working memory, less attention has been put on the question of long-term storage. Indeed, working memory cannot be reduced to a short-term memory process. During learning of a working memory task, certain aspects, labeled task-set such as the rules, expected stimuli, and motor responses can become routine and may be consolidated into a long-term memory such as an automated procedural memory (Sakai 2008; Monsell and Graham 2021). Let's take the example of a chess player. Each game is different, and the sequence of actions to checkmate the opponent will be different, but the rules of the game, the expected stimuli, and the motor responses remain the same from game to game. In our context, although the complexity of the cognitive task assigned to the mice is not comparable to that of chess, we still anticipate the development of a rule. In some studies, this procedural memory is referred to as "reference memory" in animals, which stores the rule needed to succeed during the task (Yoon et al. 2008; Miller 2013). This aspect may explain why, through daily training in a working memory task, we observe an improvement of the performance associated with a reduction of the time response, reflecting a more efficient processing of the rule contained in procedural memory (Oberauer 2006; Vandierendonck 2016; Logie et al. 2020).
 
When analyzing the structures that support working memory, most studies aim to untangle the three components of short-term memory dynamics within a single trial: encoding, maintenance, and retrieval. Spellman et al. (Spellman et al. 2015) found that direct inputs from the ventral hippocampus (vHPC) to the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) are essential for successfully encoding task-related cues in a spatial working memory task. In contrast, Wilhelm et al. (Wilhelm et al. 2023) identified the direct projection from the mPFC to the dorsomedial striatum as crucial for maintaining spatial working memory, and Akhlaghpour et al. (Akhlaghpour et al. 2016) found that neurons from the dorsomedial striatum were sequentially activated throughout the course of the delay period.
 
Broadly, the involvement of mPFC has been described during each of these phases (Vogel et al. 2022), classifying it as a key structure supporting the short-term component (Uylings et al. 2003; Yang et al. 2014) and executive functions such as decision-making, attention, and behavioral flexibility of working memory (Ragozzino et al. 1999; Delatour and Gisquet-Verrier 2000; Gisquet-Verrier and Delatour 2006; Euston et al. 2012).
 
Regarding the mPFC subregions, Killcross et al. (Killcross 2003) distinguish between the prelimbic mPFC, which is responsible for the performance of voluntary response (goal-directed), and the infralimbic mPFC, which is involved in the incremental ability to develop habits that are no longer voluntary or goal-directed via prolonged training.
 
However, few studies address the question of the long-term storage of working memory after overtraining. Yoon et al. (Yoon et al. 2008) demonstrated the role of the dorsal hippocampus in the “reference memory” versus the mPFC in choice accuracy in a working memory task (Euston et al. 2012).
 
Although the role of the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) is well-documented for its role in inflexible automated behavior (Packard and Knowlton 2002; Yin et al. 2004; Balleine and O'Doherty 2010; Hilario et al. 2012), its implication in the context of “automated procedural memory” or “reference memory” in working memory tasks remains elusive. Thus, here, we tested whether the DLS could be at play in this context. To test this hypothesis, we trained mice in a delayed non-match-to-place (DNMP) spatial working memory task in a T-maze and discriminated between two distinct phases of memory, which are the learning and the maintenance phases. Then, using chemogenetic tools, we silenced neurons of the mPFC or DLS during either the learning phase or the maintenance phase and examined its outcomes on memory. Our results showed that, as expected, the mPFC is key for learning, but surprisingly its inhibition during maintenance had no effect on memory performance. In contrast, inhibition of the DLS affected maintenance but not learning. Using a devaluation test, we demonstrated that the learning phase is goal-directed, while the maintenance phase operates automatically and is independent of reward value. Interestingly, inhibiting the DLS during the maintenance phase did not affect memory performance, potentially because of the re-engagement of goal-directed behavior under the control of the mPFC. This possibility was further supported by the observation that simultaneous inhibition of the mPFC and DLS during the maintenance phase impaired memory performance. Thus, we show that there is a reorganization of brain circuits, with the possibility of dormant memory traces in latent circuits that can be instantly reactivated as soon as the secondary circuit, which has taken over, fails.

## Materials and methods
 
### Animals
 
Experiments were performed on forty-eight C57BL/6JRj male mice (Janvier Labs, France) aged 7 weeks at the beginning of experiments. Animals were housed in groups of six in a standard laboratory facility throughout the experiment. During the 1-h devaluation session, they were housed individually in separate cages to control food intake. The experiments were carried out with precise control of the diurnal cycle (12 h L:D without inversion) and temperature (fixed at 22 °C). Animals had free access to food and water until the beginning of the protocol.
 
### Virus and clozapine n-oxide treatment
 
A modified clozapine N-oxide (CNO)-activated human muscarinic receptor, hM4Di, was selectively expressed in neurons with a red fluorescent tag (mCherry) using an adeno-associated viral expression system (pAAV-hSyn-hM4Di-mCherry) from Addgene. Virus titer was 10E12 particles/ml. In the presence of CNO, hM4Di hyperpolarizes neurons by activating potassium channels via G protein activation, consequently inhibiting neuronal firing (Armbruster et al. 2007).
 
CNO was prepared in a saline solution (0.9% NaCl) at 1 mg/kg and injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) 30 min before the behavioral tasks to activate hM4Di and thus silence virally-transduced neurons. Control animals were injected with saline (10 ml/kg).
 
### Stereotaxic injection
 
Three weeks before behavioral experiments, the viral solution was injected into either the mPFC or the DLS, or both, by stereotaxic injection. This surgery was performed using a stereotaxic frame under general anesthesia with intraperitoneal xylazine and ketamine (10 mg/kg and 150 mg/kg, respectively). The virus was injected bilaterally at 150 nl per site in the mPFC and/or 350 nl per site in the DLS with a flow rate of 100 nl/min. Cannulas were left in place for another 5 min to avoid backflow.
 
### Histology
 
At the end of behavioral protocols, mice were sacrificed by intra-aortic perfusion of 4% paraformaldehyde under deep anesthesia for brain collection and localization of mCherry-tagged cells. Brains were then cut coronally with a vibratome at 40 μm. Slices were stained for 10 min with DAPI and mounted in Vectashield solution. Slices were imaged with a slide scanner using a 10x/0.2 objective. Mice included in the analyses showed at least 50% viral expression coverage of the targeted region (DLS and/or mPFC).
 
### Delayed no-match to place
 
All behavioral tasks were performed in a T-maze apparatus. This maze consisted of a starting box containing clean bedding and closed by a removable door. It had a central stem of 35 cm and two arms perpendicular at the end of the central stem, each of 35 cm length. The width of the arms was 5 cm. The walls of the T-maze were made of transparent Plexiglas, allowing the animal to see external cues. At each end of the two perpendicular arms, a small metal bowl contained a food reward for the rewarded phase of the behavioral protocol. Visual cues were disposed around the maze for spatial orientation. The apparatus was in an isolated room with dim lighting to avoid stressing the animals.
 
Animals were habituated to the experimental procedure over 2 days. On the first day, mice were placed in groups in the T-maze to freely explore the maze for 10 min. The floor of the maze was covered with food reward to encourage the animals to enter the T-maze. The second day was identical to the first day, but animals were placed individually in the maze for the 10-min exploratory phase, and food was only at the reward place in the metal bowl at the end of both arms. Mice were gradually food-restricted to maintain their motivation, ensuring that body weight never fell below 85% of their baseline free-feeding body weight.
 
For DNMP training, each trial was divided into two phases separated by a delay. During the sample phase, one of the two arms was blocked, forcing the mouse to move to the arm that remained open. During the choice phase, both arms were open, and the mouse had to choose to move to either arm. The trial was considered correct if the mouse visited the previously closed arm. If the mouse made the correct choice, it received a food reward. The delay between the two phases was set at 90 s for the whole duration of the experiment. These two phases were repeated 10 times per day per mouse. Within a trial, the blocked arm during the sample phase was decided randomly and changed between trials. This sequence of 10 trials was itself randomly determined and changed each day. For the full protocol, an animal was considered to have reached the success criterion when it obtained an average of 8 out of 10 successful attempts (80% success rate) during three consecutive days. Based on this criterion, the task was separated into two parts: the learning phase and the maintenance phase of memory.
 
### Devaluation protocol in T-maze
 
The devaluation procedure involved two criteria based on the T-maze success score to separate two groups subject to devaluation. One group of mice was devalued during the learning phase, and one group during the maintenance phase. For the first group, animals had to achieve a 70% success rate during 2 non-consecutive days before being devalued. For the second group, animals had to first achieve the success criterion used in the DNMP (80% success rate) before devaluation. Each of the two groups was then subjected to a two-day devaluation extinction test of a specific type of reward, during which the behavior in the T-maze for the DNMP was measured. On the day following the last day of training, each animal was singly housed during 1 h to give free access to either reward food or usual food diet for 1 h. Food consumption was measured for both reward food (devalued) or cage food (non-devalued). Immediately after this pre-feeding treatment, animals were trained in the T-maze for a 10-trial devaluation session in which correct choices in the DNMP were measured in the absence of reward. A second test was conducted the following day. The two successive devaluations were separated by 24 h of starving to reverse the effect of the first devaluation before the second devaluation.
 
### Locomotor activity
 
An open-field apparatus was used to measure locomotor activity and stress. Each mouse was placed in a 40 × 40 cm square open field with an ambient light of 200 lux for 5 min and left to explore freely. Mice movements were recorded using a video camera placed above the apparatus. Distance traveled, times in the central zone, and peripheries zone were automatically analyzed.
 
### Anxiety test
 
An O-maze apparatus was used to assess anxiety levels. The O-maze test was conducted in a white circular runway raised 60 cm above the floor. The corridor width was 5 cm and was divided into two open quadrants (open zone) facing two enclosed quadrants (close zone) with a 15 cm high wall. Mice were placed in an open zone facing a closed zone and allowed to explore for 10 min with an ambient light of 200 lux. Time spent in open zones and close zones was automatically analyzed.
 
### Statistical analysis
 
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Normality of the distribution was first tested using a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and D'Agostino & Pearson test. Depending on the number of groups and the results of the normality test, groups were then compared using a One-sample t-test, Unpaired t-test, Paired t-test, Wilcoxon test, one-way or two-way analyses of variance followed by post hoc Sidak's test. Data in the figures are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

## Results
 
### Behavioral transition from goal-directed to automated behavior during the evolution of working memory
 
We analyzed if different types of behaviors (e.g., goal-directed or automated) were at play during the different phases of working memory processing. We adapted a devaluation task to analyze if the behavior was goal-directed or automated. For devaluation, we carried out a 1-h pre-feeding with either the food that the animal normally received in the cage or with the food used as a reward in the T-maze. After a 24-h starving period, the animals were given a second devaluation with the other type of food. We performed the devaluation two times during the DNMP task: either during the learning phase or during the maintenance phase.
 
During the learning phase, submission of mice to the devaluation protocol was associated with a drop in the success score in the T-maze task. This alteration was not due to the effect of satiety alone associated with pre-feeding, as non-devaluated mice did not exhibit altered memory. These data demonstrate that mice exhibit goal-directed behavior sensitive to the value of the reward during the learning phase of this working memory task.
 
To evaluate the type of behavioral strategy used by the mice during the maintenance phase, we performed the devaluation protocol after mice had reached a more stringent DNMP criterion (80% correct choices for 3 consecutive days). In this maintenance phase, the devaluation of the reward did not reproduce the effects observed during the learning phase in terms of the success score in the DNMP. Devaluated mice still exhibited a strong success score identical to non-devaluated mice and to the success score observed before devaluation. Our results demonstrate that, once the mice have reached the maintenance phase of the memory, mice adopt automated behavior, i.e., less sensitive to the value of the reward.
 
### Role of the medial prefrontal cortex during working memory
 
We hypothesized that the mPFC would be important during the learning phase, where goal-directed behavior is evident, while other structures, such as the striatum, might contribute to the automation of the memory during the maintenance phase. To test this hypothesis, we inhibited neuronal activity in the mPFC during the different phases of the DNMP task.
 
Viral expression was targeted to the mPFC. We inhibited neuronal firing by CNO or saline i.p. injection every day 30 min before the start of the training until one of the two groups reached the success criterion. Control (saline-injected) mice reached the stringent 80% correct choices for 3 consecutive days, while mPFC-inhibited mice failed to reach this criterion. This result confirms the necessity of mPFC neuronal activity during the learning phase of the DNMP task.
 
We then tested the involvement of the mPFC during memory maintenance. For this, we performed inhibition of the mPFC by injection of CNO for two consecutive days only after all mice had reached the stringent criterion of 80% correct choices for 3 consecutive days. Inhibition of neuronal activity in the mPFC during the maintenance phase did not have any effect on the performance in the DNMP task. These data show that inhibition of the mPFC impacts the behavior only during the learning phase, but not during the maintenance phase of the DNMP task.
 
### Role of the dorsolateral striatum during working memory
 
We set out to determine the involvement of the DLS in the automation of the behavior during the maintenance phase of the memory in the DNMP task. We inhibited neuronal activity in the DLS either throughout the learning phase or just during the maintenance phase, hypothesizing that DLS might be playing a role only during the maintenance phase.
 
When DLS inhibition occurred throughout the learning phase, this did not hinder learning of the task as mice reached the success criterion as did control saline-injected mice. When we inhibited neuronal activity in the DLS with CNO only during the maintenance phase, once the animals had reached the stringent success criterion, we also observed normal maintenance in DLS-inhibited mice. This suggests that the DLS is not necessary to encode or maintain this memory.
 
In both control and DLS-inhibited mice, we interrogated the strategy employed during the task by submitting them to the devaluation protocol during the maintenance phase. As expected, in the control saline-injected mice, devaluation had no effect on the correct choices obtained in the DNMP task, showing a shift towards automated behavior. However, when DLS was inhibited throughout learning or just during maintenance, devaluation had a significant effect on this task. These data suggest that, due to the absence of activity of the DLS, mice resorted back to a goal-directed strategy for maintenance.
 
### mPFC and DLS are both necessary during the maintenance phase of working memory
 
Considering that DLS-inhibited mice could shift back to a goal-directed strategy when DLS was inhibited, we hypothesized that, although the mPFC is not necessary to perform the task during the maintenance phase, it might not be completely disengaged and could compensate for this maintenance if necessary. We inhibited the mPFC and DLS during the maintenance phase after all mice had reached the stringent criterion of 80% correct choices for three consecutive days. As hypothesized, when both structures were inhibited, mice did not maintain the memory adequately when compared to saline-injected control mice.
 
No alterations in motor activity or anxiety-like behavior were observed in these mPFC/DLS-inhibited mice. These data argue that the mPFC is not fully disengaged for the maintenance of working memory in this task and can be re-engaged when the DLS is not available, explaining why DLS-inhibited mice could still switch back to a goal-oriented behavior.

## Discussion
 
Our study assessed the involvement of the mPFC and DLS during the learning and maintenance phases of a spatial working memory task. We show here that the mPFC, but not the DLS, was essential for learning the task. However, inhibition of the mPFC during the maintenance phase had no effect on memory performance, suggesting that another brain structure may take over. This shift suggests the potential involvement of structures involved in procedural memory, such as the DLS. Yet, inhibiting the DLS during maintenance had no effect on memory performance. Interestingly, results of the devaluation protocol performed on these DLS-inhibited mice demonstrated that these mice shifted their behavior back to a goal-directed strategy. Finally, when both the mPFC and DLS were inhibited concomitantly during the maintenance phase, mice did not manage to maintain this memory, arguing for a necessary interplay between the two structures.
 
We adapted the devaluation protocol to assess different behavioral strategies. The decrease in the DNMP score with devaluation during learning indicated that the animals adopted a goal-directed behavior. In contrast, the lack of effect of devaluation on mice during the maintenance phase suggested that the mice had adopted a more rigid automatized behavior insensitive to changes in reward value. These results indicate a transition from goal-directed behavior to automatized behavior after prolonged training.
 
With the use of chemogenetics, we analyzed which brain structures might be responsible for the evolution of strategies in this working memory. We began by conditional inactivation of the mPFC either during the learning phase or during the maintenance phase of the DNMP task. Inhibition of the mPFC during learning prevented animals from reaching the success criterion, highlighting the crucial role of this structure in learning a working memory task. Conversely, inhibition during the maintenance phase had no impact on memory performance. It appears that the mPFC, essential during the learning phase when behavior is goal-oriented, is disengaged during the maintenance phase when behavior becomes automated.
 
Our results do not exclude the possible role of the mPFC during the maintenance phase, which always engages working memory, but its role can be compensated if this mPFC is dysfunctional. This result supports the idea that task-induced overtraining favors automated strategies, allowing other structures, such as the striatum, to take over and rendering the mPFC non-essential to the task. This idea is supported by our results, which show that inhibition of the DLS during the maintenance phase prevents the formation of automated, devaluation-insensitive behavior, whereas it has no effect when this inhibition is during the learning phase.
 
Involvement of the DLS in automated behavior is well-recognized in the literature. Neuronal activity in the DLS has also been associated with a task-bracketing pattern, marking the beginning and end of action sequences in well-learned behaviors, and this activity is insensitive to devaluation. The fixed 90-s interval between the sample and test phases in our experiment likely reinforced the task-bracketing effect, which may explain the prominent role of the DLS in our task.
 
Finally, our last experiment tackled a very interesting concept. In our experiment, inhibition of the DLS did not directly affect the animal's performance during learning and maintenance. Interestingly, DLS inhibition during the maintenance re-engaged a goal-directed behavior. This shift in strategy following DLS inactivation has been observed in dual-solution plus-maze tasks, particularly when the task becomes more demanding. The re-engagement of the mPFC appears to be the most plausible hypothesis, since simultaneous inhibition of the mPFC and DLS precipitates the memory deficit. We suggest that the memory trace within the mPFC network is neither erased nor transferred to the dorsal striatum, but rather remains in a dormant or silent state—ready to be re-engaged when cognitive flexibility is required.
 
The question that arises is: why are different strategies supported by distinct neural networks? The most plausible answer is a matter of cost-benefit trade-off. This adaptation of behavioral strategies may reflect a reduction in memory load, shifting from a cortical flexible system to a more subcortical automated system. Our results support the possible coexistence of several behavioral strategies, with the most adapted, cost-effective strategy expressing itself naturally.
